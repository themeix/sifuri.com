<p>For those of you who didn’t know: </p>
<p>My older son has what I describe as mild to moderate autism. He&#8217;s happy; he smiles; he talks; he reads; he does math – add, subtract, multiply, and learning to divide. He&#8217;s very affectionate: I know he loves his brother and me, and he knows that we love him.</p>
<p>He attends a <a href="http://reedacademy.org">private nonprofit school for kids with autism</a>. Learning things is both easy for him – deep down I believe his skills are there – and hard: learning is sometimes really slow. It takes <em>a lot</em> of work for him to focus: far more than those random adult who describe themselves as &#8220;a little ADD.&#8221; His memory plays tricks on him: he was able to teach himself the sign language alphabet from a placard at the playground and the capitals of all 50 states, but at the same time he can&#8217;t reliably remember what he ate that day for lunch or dinner. In some very fundamental sense, he knows what matters to him and pretty much ignores the rest.</p>
<p>I work regularly at trying to give him more and more responsibility for his own life. After all, as I&#8217;ve written before, self-determination is the touchstone of freedom.</p>
<p>I&#8217;m not sure what his future will be, in much the same way that I don&#8217;t know whether my other son will go to Caltech or Stanford or Allegheny or MCC. But I know that my son’s life will get better; every day his future improves, and that might be all that really matters. After all, isn&#8217;t that what matters for all of our children? I&#8217;d rather they be happy freelance poets than miserable, insufferable, self-medicating, suicidal neurosurgeons (or lawyers).</p>
<p>Feel free to ask me questions. This isn’t any <a href="http://asdworld.com">big secret</a> in our lives.</p>
