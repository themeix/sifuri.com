<h2>Sometimes, the solution is right in front of you.</h2>
<p>I&#8217;m a science fiction fan; I can still recall the set of four or five vertical rotating racks of paperbacks at the Paddy Hill Library. I read most of those books, I&#8217;m sure &#8211; dozens and dozens.</p>
<p>Among all the science fiction themes, time travel is probably my favorite. I always enjoyed, and still really do, finding out how an author was going to resolve all the questions that people have about how time travel might work.</p>
<p>Most of my life, I&#8217;d heard about the Hitler question. The question is whether, if you were a time traveler and were in the right time and place, you would kill HItler. One answer is yes, he&#8217;s bad; the tension is between that and the &#8220;no, I wouldn&#8217;t, because it could unleash something much worse.&#8221; </p>
<p>Admittedly, I was stuck on this one for a long time (to be fair, I was a kid when this was already in play, so I hopefully just assumed that it was a tough problem). Then I realized that the whole premise of the question is flawed. </p>
<p>Taking a <a href="http://rickcolosimo.com/make-sure-your-decisions-lead-to-actions/">decisive</a> approach, I realized that there&#8217;s no information difference between someone from 2016 going back in time and someone deciding in 1939 to kill Hitler. In both cases, no one knows what the future will bring. Any (&amp; every) argument that supposedly suggests &#8220;don&#8217;t change the past&#8221; applies with equal force to the present, i.e., 1939, decision. Or, for that matter, to a 2016 decision to kill butchers in Daesh in Syria/Iraq. Sure, defending the weak and helpless victims of torture, slavery, and genocide might lead to some worse future, but that doesn&#8217;t mean we should abandon every moral urge we have to protect each other. </p>
<p>The principle of <a href="http://www.thoughtstorm.com/how-to-make-faster-decisions/">decisiveness</a> tells us that if a piece of information doesn&#8217;t affect the choice we might make, it turns out to be irrelevant and unnecessary. The &#8220;the future might generically/in an unforeseen way be worse&#8221; argument applies to <em>every choice</em> we make in the present and thus we disregard it and instead focus on the actual better/worse specifics of the choice. Only corporate America refuses to make decisions based on unknown unknowns (uncertainty, a la Frank Knight), and that&#8217;s because of a funky punishment/reward system that is skewed to the left (we punish people for bad outcomes without regard to whether they were the result of well-placed bets).</p>
<p>I can&#8217;t believe that in all the years I&#8217;ve read about this debate in scifi, no one&#8217;s ever raised this issue. THAT is why I do what I do: I untangle problems to create and structure opportunities for decisions.</p>
