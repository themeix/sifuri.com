<p>Someone asked me recently about being a dad, and here was my response.</p>
<blockquote><p>I think my main goal is to help them really internalize the trick of finding the upside instead of the downside, with a healthy dose of old-fashioned American &#8220;if it&#8217;s broke, fix it.&#8221; I don&#8217;t think being an optimist means you just accept whatever life dishes out &#8212; optimist is not another word for victim.</p></blockquote>
<p>Am I injecting a bit? Yes, I&#8217;m very sensitive to this issue of fundamental optimism vs. fundamental pessimism, crossed with active/passive approaches. Seems like time for an <a href="http://thisisindexed.com/">Indexed</a>-like four quadrant map. Maybe I&#8217;ll draw one today.</p>
<p>My next thoughts on this optimism/pessimism dimension were a combination of ideas:</p>
<blockquote><p>It&#8217;s in that realm where I think Buddhism doesn&#8217;t map onto American psychology as neatly as it does in other countries&#8217; cultures. (Of course, I saw a documentary film on Burma/Myanmar recently that made the argument that the deep Buddhist culture of the people made them more (too!) accepting of such crappy conditions in their country. My friend, native-born but from NorCal, called BS on that.)</p></blockquote>
<p>The movie &#8220;<a href="http://www.theycallitmyanmar.com/">They Call It Myanmar</a>&#8221; didn&#8217;t make this point explicitly, but it certainly focused on, by juxtaposition, &#8220;deep&#8221; Buddhism, guilt, acceptance, and apparent passivity.</p>
